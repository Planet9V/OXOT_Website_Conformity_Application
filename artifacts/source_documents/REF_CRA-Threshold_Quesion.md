## 21. CRA Scope Determination for SaaS — The Threshold Question

### 21.1 Legal Definitions Governing Scope

CRA Article 3(1) defines a "product with digital elements" as "any software or hardware product and its remote data processing solutions, including software or hardware components to be placed on the market separately". Article 3(2) defines "remote data processing" as "any data processing at a distance for which the software is designed and developed by the manufacturer or under the responsibility of the manufacturer, and the absence of which would prevent the product with digital elements from performing one of its functions". Recital 9 expressly removes standalone Software-as-a-Service from CRA scope, but carves back in any remote data processing that is tied to a regulated product. Pure cloud-native SaaS — consumed via browser, with no local installation, and not marketed as a distinct product accompanying hardware or downloadable software — falls outside CRA and is instead governed by NIS2 for services.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

### 21.2 The Six-Condition Test for "Remote Data Processing Solution" Status

Industry guidance (DigitalEurope, representing major EU tech manufacturers) has proposed a structured six-condition test based on Recitals 11-12 and Articles 3(1)-(2) to determine whether a cloud/SaaS backend is swept into CRA scope as part of a regulated product:[^6]

| Condition | Requirement | Practical Test |
|---|---|---|
| I1 | RDPS must be designed, developed, and applied by or on behalf of the manufacturer, specifically for the product | Is the backend purpose-built for this product, not a general-purpose third-party cloud service? |
| I2 | Data stored/collected/generated by the product is processed by the RDPS outside the product, at a distance | Does data leave the device/software and get processed remotely? |
| I3 | The remote processing/storage must be necessary for the product to perform its functions | Would the product stop working (in whole or part) without this service? |
| I4 | The RDPS's main function is remote processing/storage of data for that specific product | Is data processing for this product the primary purpose of the service, not incidental? |
| I5 | The service performing remote data processing directly interacts with the product | Is there direct technical interaction (API calls, sync) between the product and the SaaS backend? |
| I6 | The service involves bidirectional data exchange with the product | Does data flow both ways — product sends data to the SaaS, and processed results are sent back to enable functions? |

All six conditions must generally be satisfied for a cloud backend to be captured as an in-scope remote data processing solution; a general-purpose cloud service that merely hosts a product's data without a bidirectional functional dependency would not meet I3 and I6. The canonical example is a smart-home manufacturer's cloud that enables remote control of a physical device — if the cloud were unavailable, the device could not perform its remote-control function.[^2][^7][^6]

### 21.3 In-Scope vs. Out-of-Scope Scenarios for SaaS Teams

| Scenario | CRA Status | Governing Regime |
|---|---|---|
| Browser-only SaaS platform, no downloadable client, not sold as a "product" | Out of scope | NIS2 (if qualifying as essential/important entity), GDPR[^5][^3] |
| SaaS backend required for a companion mobile app tied to a hardware device (e.g., fitness tracker) | In scope as remote data processing solution | CRA (full manufacturer obligations apply to the backend as part of the product)[^8][^2] |
| SaaS platform bundled with a downloadable desktop agent that depends on the cloud to function | In scope | CRA — the downloadable agent is the "product," and the cloud backend is its remote data processing solution[^3] |
| Standalone SaaS analytics tool with no tied hardware/software product, marketed independently | Out of scope | NIS2/GDPR only[^1][^5] |
| General-purpose IaaS/PaaS provider (e.g., hosting infrastructure) used by a manufacturer, not built specifically for one product | Out of scope (fails I1/I4) | NIS2[^6][^2] |

Because this determination is fact-specific and consequential, DigitalEurope and other industry bodies are actively pressing the European Commission for formal implementing guidance to reduce ambiguity — as of mid-2026 this remains an interpretive gray zone requiring a documented internal scoping memo rather than a definitive published test.[^6]

## 22. Economic Operator Roles and Obligations (Detailed)

The CRA assigns differentiated obligations across four economic operator roles, relevant to SaaS companies that may act as manufacturer, or interact with importers/distributors/authorized representatives in their supply chain.[^9][^10]

### 22.1 Manufacturer (Heaviest Obligation Tier — Article 13)

The manufacturer bears full responsibility for: ensuring the product meets Annex I essential requirements; performing a cybersecurity risk assessment; drawing up technical documentation (Annex VII); performing conformity assessment; affixing CE marking; issuing an EU Declaration of Conformity; establishing vulnerability-handling processes including SBOM, CVD policy, and secure update distribution; and reporting under Article 14. A SaaS company becomes a "manufacturer" for CRA purposes if it designs and develops the remote data processing solution tied to its own product, or if it substantially modifies a product it distributes under its own name/trademark.[^8][^11][^12]

### 22.2 Authorized Representative (Article 18)

An authorized representative acts on behalf of a non-EU manufacturer under a written mandate, and must at minimum:[^13][^14]
- Keep the EU Declaration of Conformity and technical documentation available to market surveillance authorities for at least 10 years after market placement, or the support period, whichever is longer.
- Provide all information/documentation necessary to demonstrate conformity upon a reasoned request from a market surveillance authority.
- Cooperate with market surveillance authorities on any corrective action needed to eliminate risks posed by the product.

### 22.3 Importer (Article 19)

Before placing a product on the market, importers must verify the product underwent appropriate conformity assessment, that the manufacturer drew up the EU Declaration of Conformity, and that CE marking is correctly affixed. Importers must also ensure the product carries the manufacturer's name/trademark and contact details, and — where required — the importer's own name and contact details on the product, packaging, or documentation. If an importer has reason to believe a product is non-compliant or poses a significant cybersecurity risk, it must refrain from placing the product on the market and notify the manufacturer and relevant authorities. Industry legal analysis flags Article 19 as a "structural weak point," since importers in complex distribution chains often lack the technical visibility to independently verify a manufacturer's Annex I compliance claims.[^15][^16][^11]

### 22.4 Distributor (Article 20)

Article 20 requires distributors to act with due care and, before making a product available, verify that it bears CE marking and that the manufacturer/importer have complied with specified Article 13 and Article 19 obligations and provided all necessary documents. If a distributor believes a product or the manufacturer's processes are non-compliant, it must not make the product available until brought into conformity, and must immediately inform the manufacturer and market surveillance authorities if the product poses a significant cybersecurity risk. Upon becoming aware of a vulnerability, distributors must inform the manufacturer without undue delay. Distributors also carry an ongoing obligation to monitor the cybersecurity status of products they distribute.[^17][^15]

## 23. Administrative Fines — Full Three-Tier Structure (Article 64)

The CRA's penalty framework under Article 64 mirrors GDPR-style tiered fines, with national market surveillance authorities enforcing and always applying the higher of the flat euro amount or the percentage of global turnover:[^18][^19]

| Tier | Maximum Fine | Triggering Violations |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 (highest) | €15 million or 2.5% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher | Breach of essential cybersecurity requirements (Annex I); non-compliance with core manufacturer obligations (Article 13); non-compliance with reporting obligations (Article 14); failure to ensure product cybersecurity throughout the lifecycle. Applies equally to open-source software stewards[^18][^19][^20]. |
| Tier 2 | €10 million or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher | Non-compliance with importer obligations (Articles 18-20 as referenced); non-compliance with distributor obligations (Articles 21-23); missing/incomplete EU Declaration of Conformity (Article 28); improper CE marking use (Articles 30-32); inadequate conformity assessment procedures (Article 33); non-compliance with notified body requirements[^18][^19]. |
| Tier 3 (lowest) | €5 million or 1% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher | Providing incorrect, incomplete, or misleading information to notified bodies or market surveillance authorities[^18][^19][^20]. |

For a SaaS company whose backend qualifies as an in-scope remote data processing solution, Tier 1 exposure is the most material risk category, since Article 13/14 vulnerability-handling and reporting failures sit at the top tier alongside Annex I essential-requirement breaches.[^20][^18]

## 24. Support Period and Lifecycle Obligations (Detailed)

### 24.1 The Five-Year Floor

CRA Article 13(8) requires manufacturers to determine a support period reflecting the time the product is expected to remain in use, with a hard minimum of five years unless the product's expected lifetime is shorter. Products reasonably expected to remain in use beyond five years (e.g., industrial or infrastructure-adjacent products — directly relevant to OT/critical-infrastructure contexts) require a longer support period matched to realistic field lifespan.[^21][^22][^23]

### 24.2 Retroactive Application Risk

A commonly overlooked implication: because the support-period clock is tied to when a product was placed on the market — not when the CRA's main obligations take effect in December 2027 — products sold in earlier years may still carry live support obligations once CRA enforcement begins, if their five-year (or longer) window has not yet elapsed.[^23]

### 24.3 Documentation Retention

Technical documentation and the EU Declaration of Conformity must be retained for at least 10 years after the product is placed on the market, or for the support period, whichever is longer — this retention duty applies to manufacturers and, per Article 18(2)(a), equally to authorized representatives holding a mandate.[^14][^22][^13]

### 24.4 Free, Undelayed Security Updates

Security updates addressing Annex I compliance issues must be delivered to users free of charge and without undue delay, with no permission to paywall baseline security fixes as a premium feature.[^24][^22]

## 25. Open-Source Software Steward Obligations (Article 24)

### 25.1 Definition

An "open-source software steward" is any legal entity (explicitly not an individual, and not a manufacturer) that systematically provides sustained support for the development of specific open-source software products intended for commercial activities — this includes foundations, platform maintainers, and organizations ensuring project viability.[^25][^26]

### 25.2 Light-Touch Regime

Article 24 stewards face a materially reduced obligation set compared to manufacturers, avoiding full conformity assessment, CE marking, and complete technical documentation:[^27][^25]
- Put in place and document, in a verifiable manner, a cybersecurity policy fostering secure product development and effective vulnerability handling by the developers of the product.
- That policy must specifically address documenting, addressing, and remediating vulnerabilities, and promote information-sharing about discovered vulnerabilities within the open-source community.[^27]
- Cooperate with market surveillance authorities, upon request, to mitigate cybersecurity risks posed by an open-source product with digital elements.
- Provide the cybersecurity policy documentation to authorities upon reasoned request, in a language the authority can understand.[^27]
- Report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents impacting the security of products with digital elements, consistent with the general Article 14 obligations.[^28]

### 25.3 Fine Exemption

Per Article 64(10), open-source software stewards are explicitly not subject to administrative fines for CRA infringements, even though the Tier 1 substantive obligations under Article 24 nominally track the same severity category as manufacturer obligations. This is a deliberate policy choice to avoid discouraging non-commercial and community-driven open-source maintenance — but a SaaS company relying on OSS dependencies remains fully liable as manufacturer for its own product regardless of any steward's exemption.[^18][^28][^25]

## 26. CRA / NIS2 / GDPR Regulatory Overlap — Structured Mapping

### 26.1 High-Level Division of Regulatory Focus

CRA governs the security of products with digital elements throughout their lifecycle; NIS2 governs risk management and incident reporting obligations for designated essential/important entities providing services; GDPR governs the protection of personal data and breach notification. For a SaaS company, all three regimes typically apply simultaneously — GDPR because the SaaS processes user data, NIS2 if the company qualifies as an essential/important entity under national transposition, and CRA if any part of the offering (companion app, downloadable agent, or the backend itself if it is a qualifying remote data processing solution) is in scope.[^29][^30]

### 26.2 Detailed Cross-Regime Requirement Mapping

| Requirement Area | GDPR | NIS2 | CRA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Risk Assessment | Data protection impact assessments (DPIAs) for high-risk processing[^30] | Broader organizational cybersecurity risk management measures[^30] | Product-specific cybersecurity risk assessment under Article 13, documented in Annex VII[^30][^12] |
| Incident Reporting | Personal data breach notification to supervisory authority (72 hours) and affected individuals[^30] | Significant incident reporting to national CSIRT (early warning 24h, notification 72h, final report 1 month)[^30] | Actively exploited vulnerability / severe incident reporting via ENISA Single Reporting Platform (24h/72h/14 days-1 month)[^30] |
| Security Measures | Technical/organizational measures protecting personal data specifically[^30] | Comprehensive organizational and network security measures[^30] | Comprehensive product-level security-by-design and secure-by-default requirements (Annex I)[^30] |
| Documentation | Records of processing activities (Article 30 GDPR)[^30] | Extended cybersecurity policy and governance documentation[^30] | Full technical documentation package (Annex VII) including SBOM, architecture, test reports[^30][^12] |
| Monitoring | Ongoing data processing oversight and DPO function[^30] | System-wide security monitoring across the organization[^30] | Product-specific post-market monitoring plan and vulnerability register[^30][^31] |

Academic mapping analysis (2025) identifies specific technical overlaps between CRA and GDPR spanning confidentiality/integrity/availability guarantees, data minimization principles, traceability requirements, data erasure capability, and security testing obligations — suggesting a single unified control framework can satisfy overlapping evidence requirements across both regimes if designed deliberately rather than building three parallel compliance programs.[^32][^29]

## 27. Conformity Assessment Modules — Detailed Mechanics

### 27.1 Module A — Internal Control (Self-Assessment)

Applicable to Default-category products and Important Class I products where a harmonised standard, common specification, or certification of at least "substantial" assurance level has been applied. The manufacturer independently assesses conformity against Annex I, compiles technical documentation, and issues the EU Declaration of Conformity without third-party involvement.[^33][^34]

### 27.2 Module B + C — EU-Type Examination Plus Production Control

A two-phase procedure defined in Parts II and III of Annex VIII. In Module B, a notified body performs an EU-type examination of the product's design, cybersecurity controls, and vulnerability-handling processes, issuing an EU-type examination certificate if compliant. In Module C, the manufacturer then ensures ongoing production units conform to the certified type through internal production controls, documenting production processes and maintaining quality controls, referencing the original certificate when signing the EU Declaration of Conformity and applying CE marking. Manufacturers must report changes affecting the certified type back to the notified body and may need recertification for substantial changes.[^35][^36][^33]

### 27.3 Module H — Full Quality Assurance

A single continuous-surveillance route in which a notified body approves the manufacturer's cybersecurity quality management system covering design, development, and production, then conducts ongoing surveillance audits of that system rather than approving one static product design. This module is mandatory for Important Class II products (e.g., firewalls, IDS/IPS, hypervisors, container runtimes) and is an available option for Critical-class products alongside EU Common Criteria certification.[^36][^37][^33]

### 27.4 Choosing the Applicable Module

CRA Article 32 states the manufacturer may choose from: (a) internal control based on Module A; (b) EU-type examination (Module B) followed by conformity to type based on internal production control (Module C); (c) full quality assurance (Module H); or (d) where available, a European cybersecurity certification scheme under Article 27(9). The choice narrows sharply once a product is classified Important Class II or Critical, where third-party notified body involvement (Module B+C or H, or EUCC) becomes mandatory rather than optional.[^37][^34][^33]

## 28. Technical Documentation (Annex VII) — Full Content Requirements

Article 31 and Annex VII specify the mandatory contents of the technical documentation file, which forms the evidentiary backbone for both self-assessment and notified-body review:[^12]

1. **General product description**: intended purpose; software versions affecting compliance with essential cybersecurity requirements; for hardware, photographs/illustrations of external features, markings, and internal layout; user information and instructions per Annex II.[^12]
2. **Design, development, production, and vulnerability-handling description**: system architecture explaining how software components build on and integrate with each other; specifications of vulnerability-handling processes including the SBOM, the coordinated vulnerability disclosure policy, evidence of a published contact address for vulnerability reporting, and a description of the technical solution used for secure update distribution; specifications and validation of production/monitoring processes.[^12]
3. **Cybersecurity risk assessment**: an assessment of the risks the product is designed, developed, produced, delivered, and maintained against, per Article 13, including how each Annex I Part I essential requirement is applicable.[^12]
4. **Support period justification**: the information taken into account to determine the support period under Article 13(8).[^12]
5. **Standards and specifications list**: harmonised standards applied (in full or in part) and published in the Official Journal, common specifications under Article 27, or certification schemes applied under Regulation (EU) 2019/881; where none were applied, a description of the alternative solutions adopted to meet Annex I Parts I and II, including any other relevant technical specifications used.[^12]
6. **Test reports**: verifying conformity of both the product and its vulnerability-handling processes against Annex I Parts I and II.[^12]
7. **EU Declaration of Conformity copy**.[^12]
8. **SBOM**: provided where applicable, further to a reasoned request from a market surveillance authority, if necessary for that authority to verify Annex I compliance.[^12]

### 28.1 Extended Practical Breakdown (Industry Compilation)

Consultancy compilations further decompose the Annex VII requirement into ten discrete evidentiary documents useful for building an internal compliance checklist:[^38]

| Document | Corresponding Annex VII Point | Content Summary |
|---|---|---|
| Product Background Description | General Description | Product identity, intended purpose, versions[^38] |
| Cybersecurity Architecture and Design | Point 2(a) | System architecture diagrams, logical data-flow diagrams, component integration explanation[^38] |
| Production, Release, and Monitoring Process | Point 2(c) | Secure production-line coding practices, supply-chain compromise prevention, post-market threat monitoring[^38] |
| Vulnerability Handling Process | Point 2(b) | Full vulnerability-handling workflow documentation[^38] |
| Cybersecurity Risk Assessment | Point 3 | Formal risk assessment output[^38] |
| Support Period Evidence | Point 4 | Documented basis for the determined support period[^38] |
| Standards List | Point 5 | Applied harmonised standards/common specifications[^38] |
| Test Reports | Point 6 | Security testing evidence[^38] |
| SBOM | Point 8 | Full software bill of materials[^38] |
| EU Declaration of Conformity | Point 7 | Signed conformity declaration[^38] |

### 28.2 Post-Market Surveillance File Contents (Annex VII Section 4 Practical Detail)

Beyond the pre-market technical file, ongoing post-market evidence must be maintained:[^31]
- **Post-market monitoring plan**: security monitoring strategy, threat intelligence sources, automated tooling and manual processes, alert prioritization methodology.[^31]
- **Vulnerability database/register**: discovered vulnerabilities with discovery date, reporting date, remediation date, impact assessment, and record of CSIRT/ENISA interactions where applicable.[^31]
- **Incident reporting documentation**: incident timeline, exploitation evidence, mitigation actions, notices sent to authorities, user notifications where applicable.[^31]
- **Update and patch history**: version history, security patches released with rationale, and validation steps performed for each update.[^31]

## 29. Consolidated SaaS CRA Compliance Checklist

Drawing together Sections 21–28, a SaaS organization should work through the following sequence:

1. Complete and document a formal scoping determination against the six-condition remote-data-processing test (Section 21.2); retain this analysis as evidence of a good-faith compliance effort even if the conclusion is "out of scope."
2. If in scope, confirm economic operator role(s) — most SaaS companies building their own tied product/backend will be the manufacturer (Section 22.1).
3. Classify the product under Annex III/IV (Default, Important Class I/II, or Critical) to determine which conformity assessment module applies (Section 27).
4. Build the Annex VII technical documentation file, prioritizing the SBOM, vulnerability-handling process description, and CVD policy since these feed directly into both conformity assessment and Article 14 reporting readiness (Section 28).
5. Establish the support period determination and retention plan (minimum 5 years support, minimum 10 years or support period for documentation) (Section 24).
6. Map overlapping GDPR/NIS2 obligations into a single unified evidence framework rather than duplicating effort across three compliance programs (Section 26).
7. If dependent on open-source components maintained by a steward organization, confirm the steward's Article 24 policy exists, while recognizing this does not reduce the SaaS company's own manufacturer-level obligations (Section 25).
8. Quantify Tier 1 financial exposure (up to €15M / 2.5% global turnover) in any internal business case for CRA program investment, since Article 13/14 failures — the core of the PSIRT and SBOM programs already documented in Sections 1–20 — carry the highest penalty tier (Section 23).
